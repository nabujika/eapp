Introduction

Why Health Economics Defines Efficiency Differently

In classical economics, efficiency often means either maximizing output from a given set of inputs or minimizing cost for a given output. Health systems need a wider definition because healthcare is shaped by information asymmetry, externalities, and the social judgment that care should not depend only on ability to pay.

The Standard Economics Definition

A textbook production frontier asks whether a system is getting the highest possible output from its resources. That logic still matters, but it is not enough on its own for public health.

Why Health Systems Are Different

Patients cannot fully judge quality, preventive care generates spillovers beyond the individual, and public systems are expected to protect access even when market demand is weak.

A Three-Lens Framework

Health economists therefore separate efficiency into technical, allocative, and governance lenses, with equity and financial risk acting as a binding constraint on any narrow cost-saving view.

Technical Efficiency

More Output From the Same Input - Or the Same Output at Lower Cost

Technical efficiency asks how close a production unit is to the best-practice frontier. If one public facility can achieve the same output with fewer beds, staff, or rupees than another, it is operating more efficiently.

Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is useful here because it builds the frontier from observed units without forcing a single production function on a heterogeneous health system.

This section intentionally keeps the evidence focused on productivity and frontier distance. Broader spending figures from the slides have been removed because they describe system scale, not technical efficiency itself.

Allocative Efficiency

Putting Resources Into the Service Mix With the Highest Social Return

Allocative efficiency asks whether spending is directed toward the right services. A technically efficient system can still underperform if the budget is tilted toward lower-value uses while prevention and primary care remain underfunded.

In this lens, the relevant statistics are not about hospital productivity. They are about service mix, opportunity cost, and whether the budget aligns with the highest marginal social benefit.

The presentation's evidence points to a recurring structural issue: public spending remains heavily weighted toward curative and tertiary care even though policy targets emphasize primary care.

Governance Efficiency

How Much of the Budget Actually Reaches Patients

Governance efficiency captures the gap between money authorized and money effectively translated into care. Even a technically sound and well-allocated plan can fail if procurement, payroll, transfers, and monitoring leak value along the way.

This is where principal-agent problems matter. When the principal cannot fully observe effort, price, or compliance, agents can shirk or extract rents.

The statistics kept here focus on leakage, absenteeism, and fund utilization - all direct governance indicators rather than outcome or spending-mix measures.

Effective Spending After Leakage

Eeff = B (1 - δ)

δ = Σ (Li / B)

Li = procurement leakage + absenteeism + ghost workers

If δ = 0.25, then only 75 percent of each budgeted rupee reaches the point of care.

Equity & Financial Risk

Efficiency Without Equity Is Incomplete

A system is not meaningfully efficient if it cuts public cost by shifting risk onto households. Health economics therefore treats equity and financial protection as a binding constraint, not a decorative add-on.

In this section, the relevant evidence is household burden: out-of-pocket spending, catastrophic expenditure, poverty effects, and the distribution of that burden across income groups.

These are kept separate from governance and allocative statistics because they answer a different question: who pays when the system underperforms?

Synthesis

Three Lenses, One System

Once efficiency is defined across production, allocation, execution, and household protection, the policy question becomes sharper: does public spending in India translate into better health outcomes, or do bottlenecks shift across these lenses?

Lens Core question Clear equation Relevant statistic kept
Technical Are inputs used without avoidable waste? TE = θ*, TE ∈ [0,1] Average TE score is about 0.55 across public PHCs
Allocative Is the budget going to the highest-value mix of services? MBi / ci = MBj / cj Primary care remains near 21 percent versus a much higher policy target

Key takeaway

A larger budget alone does not guarantee efficiency. The system has to use inputs productively, fund the right mix of care, deliver money cleanly, and protect households from financial shock.
