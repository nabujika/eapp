Introduction

Defining Efficiency in India's Public Health Sector

Efficiency in public health is not just lower cost. It is the ability to convert inputs into outcomes, direct budgets toward high-value care, deliver funds without leakage, and protect households from financial shock.

Standard economics

Output from inputs

Classical efficiency asks whether a system maximizes output from a fixed bundle of labor, capital, and spending.

Why health is different

Care has spillovers and asymmetry

Patients cannot perfectly judge quality, prevention benefits others, and public systems are expected to preserve access when markets would undersupply it.

Working definition

Efficiency needs multiple lenses

Health economists separate production, allocation, execution, and household protection because a system can perform well on one dimension and fail on another.

02

Technical Efficiency

Technical efficiency asks whether the same inputs could produce more health output

A facility is technically efficient when its mix of staff, beds, and budget is converted into the maximum feasible output relative to comparable units.

DEA frontier formulation

θ* = min θ

subject to

Xλ ≤ θx0

Yλ ≥ y0, λ ≥ 0

TE = θ* and TE ∈ [0, 1]

DEA is useful because it builds a best-practice frontier from observed facilities without forcing one production function on a heterogeneous public system.

Allocative Efficiency

Allocative efficiency asks whether public money is going to the highest social return

A system can be productive and still underperform if too much spending is concentrated in lower-value curative care while primary and preventive services remain underfunded.

Social welfare allocation rule

max W = Σ piqi

subject to

Σ ciqi ≤ B

Optimal when MBi / ci = MBj / cj

Allocative efficiency is reached when each rupee delivers the same marginal social benefit across competing services.

Governance Efficiency

Governance efficiency is the gap between money authorized and care actually delivered

Procurement failures, absenteeism, weak monitoring, and delayed transfers all shrink the share of the budget that reaches patients at the point of care.

Effective spending after leakage

Eeff = B (1 - δ)

δ = Σ (Li / B)

Li = procurement leakage + absenteeism + ghost workers

If leakage equals 25 percent, only three-quarters of each budgeted rupee reaches the delivery chain.

05

Equity & Financial Risk

Efficiency is incomplete if public savings simply become household health shocks

Health systems are not meaningfully efficient when lower public spending is offset by out-of-pocket payments, catastrophic expenditure, or poverty caused by healthcare costs.

Catastrophic expenditure rule

CHE = 1 if OOP / Chh > κ

κ = 10% under the threshold used in the presentation

Financial risk = E[CHE] across income groups

Equity gap = CHEpoor / CHErich

A lower public budget is not an efficiency gain when households absorb the gap through private medical spending.

06

Synthesis

Efficiency improves only when production, allocation, execution, and protection work together

Bigger budgets alone do not guarantee better outcomes. The public system has to use inputs well, buy the right mix of care, keep funds intact through delivery, and prevent financial harm to households.

Technical

Use inputs without avoidable waste

TE = θ*

Average TE remains around 0.55 across public PHCs.

Allocative

Back the highest-value mix of care

MBi / ci = MBj / cj

Primary care remains near 21 percent of government health spending.

Governance

Keep public funds reaching patients

Eeff = B (1 - δ)

Leakage and absenteeism reduce the effective value of each rupee.

Equity & risk

Protect households from forced private spending

CHE = 1 if OOP / Chh > κ

Out-of-pocket burden is still 48.2 percent of total health expenditure.

Bottom line

Efficiency is system quality under constraint

In India's public health sector, efficiency should be judged by whether the system turns scarce resources into accessible, high-value, well-governed care without shifting risk onto households.
